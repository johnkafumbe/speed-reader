
Photosynthesis is a set of biological processes used by organisms such as plants, algae, and cyanobacteria 
to convert light energy (often sunlight)into chemical energy that powers metabolism. In the most common form—oxygenic photosynthesis—oxygen 
is released as a byproduct. The captured energy ends up stored in the bonds of organic compounds (often carbohydrates
such as sugars and starches).

During the light-dependent reactions, energy from light is used to strip electrons from suitable substances such as water, producing oxygen gas. 
This process also generates energy-carrying molecules including ATP and NADPH.

In plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, sugars are synthesized in a later set of reactions often described as light-independent,
including the Calvin cycle, which incorporates carbon dioxide into existing organic compounds 
and—using ATP and NADPH—produces carbohydrates like glucose.

Light-dependent Reactions:

In the light-dependent reactions, one molecule of the pigment chlorophyll absorbs one photon and loses one electron. 
This electron is taken up by a modified form of chlorophyll called pheophytin, which passes the electron to a quinone molecule, 
starting the flow of electrons down an electron transport chain that leads to the ultimate reduction of NADP to NADPH. 
In addition, this creates a proton gradient (energy gradient) across the chloroplast membrane, which is used by ATP 
synthase in the synthesis of ATP. The chlorophyll molecule ultimately regains the electron it lost when a water molecule is 
split in a process called photolysis, which releases oxygen.
